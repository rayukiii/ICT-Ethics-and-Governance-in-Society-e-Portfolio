## Artefact 1: What is Automated Decision Making?

<img width="1611" height="891" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8f263daf-b8b8-4ca7-afbd-52831a91391e" />
https://www.ombudsman.gov.au/about/education-events-and-resources#:~:text=Automated%20Decision%20Making%20Better%20Practice%20Guide

### Summary of the artefact

This guide from the Commonwealth Ombudsman (2025, p.7) explains how automated systems can carry out part or all of an administrative decision-making process.
Although Automated Decision Making (ADM) can be used by both governments and businesses, this guide focuses on government agencies. 
There are some systems follow fixed rules, such as calculating a payment using a formula, while others use machine learning to make predictions.
They can make decisions, recommend an outcome, provide summaries or gather information that helps a person decide (Commonwealth Ombudsman 2025, p.7).
What I found interesting was that a system does not need to make the final decision to influence the outcome.
Even the information it provides can affect what a person decides.
Overall, I learnt that Automated Decision Making (ADM) includes different types of technology and different levels of human involvement.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this artefact because it explains the different ways automated systems can take part in government decisions.
Before reading this artefact, I mainly thought about automation through examples such as immigration gates, which I often encounter whenever I travel.
The guide reminded me of how I already use ChatGPT on my daily life. What was new to me was learning how this kind of support can form part of government decision-making processes, 
including systems that follow fixed rules rather than using AI (Commonwealth Ombudsman 2025, p.7).
Additionally, this also reminded me of my previous e-portfolio on censorship, where I discussed how ordinary users have a responsibility to report harmful content.
Similarly, I believe we should speak up when we notice incorrect information or unfair outcomes from automated systems.
However, reporting a problem would not mean much if the organisation simply ignored it.
The people responsible should listen, investigate and correct any mistakes instead of assuming that the system must be right.
Overall, this artefact helped me understand that responsible use of ADM involves both people raising concerns and organisations taking those concerns seriously.

## Artefact 2: Learning from Robodebt

<img width="1880" height="1047" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/eb6040d2-21be-4bdc-a15b-be44b36df35b" />
https://ministers.pmc.gov.au/gallagher/2024/update-robodebt-royal-commission-reforms

### Summary of the artefact
This article explains the Australian Government’s progress in responding to the Robodebt Royal Commission.
It acknowledges the harm caused by Robodebt and outlines reforms intended to prevent similar failures.
These include changes to debt management that consider people’s individual circumstances and ending the use of external debt collectors (Gallagher 2024).
As this article presents the government’s own account of its progress, I think it is useful for understanding its response, but it does not prove that every problem has been resolved.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this artefact because learning about Robodebt challenged my earlier view that automated systems were mostly helpful.
What affected me most was hearing about the suicides discussed in connection with the scheme.
It made me think about how a government decision could affect someone beyond their finances, especially when they are already struggling and feel that nobody believes them.
Our Week 10 material also discusses how incorrect debts and ignored warnings caused serious harm (CQUniversity 2026a).
However, I still believe automated systems have a place in government. 
Human decision-makers can also make mistakes because of fatigue, bias or personal interests, so removing automation would not automatically make decisions fairer.
For me, the lesson from Robodebt is that supporting technology should not mean defending it when there is evidence that something is wrong.
If a system is causing harm, the people responsible should be willing to stop using it and investigate, even if it saves time or money.
This connects with the ACS value of prioritising the public interest, because protecting people should come before protecting an organisation’s reputation or meeting its targets (Australian Computer Society 2014).

## Artefact 3: Protecting People’s Rights in Automated Decisions

<img width="1892" height="1043" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/acf67964-ce62-4d89-9abb-5301dddc4752" />
https://humanrights.gov.au/resource-hub/by-resource-type/submissions/use-of-automated-decision-making-by-government

### Summary of the artefact
This artefact presents the Australian Human Rights Commission’s (2025) recommendations on the use of ADM by government agencies.
It discusses how people’s rights should be protected when automated systems are involved in decisions that affect them.
The Commission recommends assessing possible human rights impacts, informing people when ADM significantly influences a decision, and providing reasons for the outcome.
It also proposes an independent AI Commissioner to help oversee the use of AI.
What stood out to me was the focus on giving people information about decisions made about them.
These recommendations show how government use of ADM could be improved, although the submission itself does not make them legal requirements.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this artefact because I agree that governments should assess possible human rights impacts before using ADM.
As a future ICT professional, I would feel responsible if a system I helped develop unfairly harmed someone.
Those affected could be people I love or people I have never met.
My empathy would not allow me to ignore their wellbeing just to protect my own interests.
This connects with the ACS principle of prioritising the public interest (Australian Computer Society 2014).
If my employer wanted to release a system with known problems that could harm people, I would raise my concerns and push to delay its release until those problems were addressed.
For me, professional responsibility includes considering what the technology could do to people’s lives.
Although these proposals are recommendations, I believe protections like these should become legal requirements wherever governments use ADM.

### Summary of the artefact
These sources explain how Singapore and the Philippines are developing automated immigration clearance.
Singapore’s Immigration & Checkpoints Authority (2024) describes how foreign visitors arriving at Changi Airport can use automated lanes without enrolling beforehand.
It explains the use of facial and iris recognition to verify travellers’ identities, alongside checks that help officers identify people who need further examination.
Meanwhile, the Philippine Information Agency (2024) reports the expansion of e-gates as part of efforts to reduce processing times.
These examples show how different governments are introducing automation into a service that many travellers encounter.
They also helped me understand that automated clearance involves more than simply scanning a passport and opening a gate.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose these sources because I have personally used automated immigration gates in Singapore, making this topic easier for me to connect with.
I usually feel more comfortable using the device than answering questions directly from an immigration officer.
Before studying ADM, I mostly thought about that feeling of relief and how convenient the process was.
Reading about the checks behind it helped me understand how technology and officers share different parts of the work.
The comparison with the Philippines also showed me that this development extends beyond one country, although I should not assume that every country’s system works in exactly the same way.
This connects with the ACS value of enhancing quality of life, which includes understanding the needs of people affected by technology (Australian Computer Society 2014).
For me, a successful service should make travel easier while providing help when someone cannot complete the automated process.
Having officers nearby is part of why I feel comfortable trusting the gates.

## Artefact 5: Predictive Policing in China

<img width="1917" height="1056" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/3f5f9ba5-7c2f-42e5-aaaf-6eca38f80394" />
https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/spotlights/2025/silicon-valley-enabled-brutal-mass-detention-and-surveillance-in-china-internal-documents-show/

### Summary of the artefact
This Associated Press investigation examines how technology companies helped develop surveillance and predictive policing systems in China.
It describes systems that analyse communications, payments, travel records and other information to identify people considered suspicious.
The investigation reports that these technologies have also been used to monitor minorities and restrict people who challenge authorities (Kang & Grauer 2025).
The connection to ADM is in how the collected information is analysed to guide decisions about who receives police attention.
This helped me understand the difference between a camera recording an event and a system using information to predict someone’s behaviour.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this artefact because I was curious about whether extensive security and surveillance could prevent crime.
My first question was why crime would still happen with so much technology involved.
However, the investigation made me consider another issue: what happens to someone who is identified as a threat before doing anything wrong?
I understand why police would investigate a warning, but I would not consider a prediction enough to treat someone as guilty.
This reminded me of my earlier privacy reflection about what happens to personal information after it is collected.
Here, the concern extends to how ordinary activities could be interpreted when different pieces of information are combined.
It also connects with Kantian ethics, because protecting society should still involve respecting people’s dignity rather than treating them only as a means to achieve security (CQUniversity 2026b).
This artefact helped me recognise that ADM can affect someone’s freedom through the attention it directs towards them, even before any final decision is made.

## Artefact 6: The Moral Cost of Automated Decisions

<img width="1915" height="1052" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ba9fa106-fc97-46f5-a421-3a1b2598b761" />
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IJSmbAY3hVk

### Summary of the artefact
In this TEDx talk, Adam Dunigan discusses how AI is being used in warfare and state surveillance, drawing on his experience in the military and intelligence work.
He raises a question that I found concerning: what happens when a system is involved in a decision that takes someone’s life, but the people involved say it was not their responsibility?
Through his discussion of autonomous weapons, he argues that automation can make people feel disconnected from the consequences of their decisions.
He also discusses how technology developed for military purposes can be used to monitor ordinary citizens, which reminded me of the surveillance issues in China from Artefact 5.
However, his message is not only about what could go wrong.
He encourages people to take responsibility in their workplaces and communities and to put human dignity before convenience or profit (Dunigan 2026).
What stood out to me was his point that even as technology becomes more capable, people still have choices about how it is developed and used.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this video because it continued the questions I had after looking at automated surveillance in China.
That artefact made me think about the people being monitored, while this talk made me consider the people behind the decisions.
Dunigan questions how responsibility can become unclear when AI is involved and everyone sees themselves as only one part of the process (Dunigan 2026).
This stood out to me because a system does not appear or get used on its own. People develop it, decide its purpose and choose how to act on its results.
Looking back across all my portfolios, I have realised that developing technology also means making choices about what we consider right and wrong.
The ethical theories we studied do not always give the same answer, but they helped me explain my reasons instead of relying only on what feels right to me.
I now understand why laws, professional codes and personal values all matter when deciding how technology should be used.
For me, progress should mean improving people’s lives and being able to explain our choices, rather than simply creating something because we can.
