## Artefact 1: What is Intellectual Property?

### Summary of the artefact
This YouTube video by the Canadian Intellectual Property Office (2018) explains what intellectual property (IP) is and why it is important. 
The video explains that intellectual property is the result of human creativity and innovation, such as inventions, technologies, artistic works, designs and other creations. 
It also introduces different forms of intellectual property protection, including copyright, patents, trademarks and trade secrets. 
One thing I found interesting was that intellectual property is treated as a valuable form of property even though it is not something physical. 
For example, a creator can have legal rights over a song, an inventor can protect an invention through a patent, and a business can protect its brand through a trademark. 
The video also explains that protecting IP can allow creators and businesses to benefit financially from their work and prevent others from using it without permission.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this video because although it was 8 years old, it gives a simple yet detailed introduction to intellectual property and helped me understand more about the basic concepts discussed in the workshop. 
Before the workshop, I only knew about ownership with physical things that one could buy or possess. 
This video helped me to completely understand what we discuss in the workshop that ideas and creative work also have their own legal and financial value and that they can be protected. 
For example, we discussed how trademarks can protect recognizable brands such as Coca-Cola, while trade secrets can protect valuable information such as a company's recipe or formula. 
We also discussed how creators have rights over how their work is used and where it can be used. 
Additionally, after going through this artefact I also found out that if you own an Intellectual property, its protection are different internationally (IPAustralia 2022). 
Overall, both of the videos made me realize that intellectual property is not just about preventing people from copying someone else's work. 
It can also be considered as an important financial or business asset, which also reminded me of an example given during the workshop about Michael Jackson, 
where creative works can continue to have significant value even after the creator has already died.

## Artefact 2: AI companies using Australian content to train AI

### Summary of the artefact
This article from ABC News, written by Worthington and Marchese (2026), reports that Australian artists have discovered their music being used in datasets to train overseas AI models without their consent or compensation. 
The article also discusses the Australian Government's response, including plans to establish an Office of AI to develop standards around artificial intelligence. 
However, experts say there are still many unanswered questions about how AI companies will disclose the data they use and how creators can be paid fairly (Worthington & Marchese 2026).
I was surprised to learn that many Australian artists I follow have become victims of this situation. 
It made me wonder how other countries are also handling this issue. I also found it interesting that Playlunch, a Melbourne-based band, had their music included in these AI training datasets. 
Since I live in Melbourne, this made the issue feel more relevant to me because it showed that AI and intellectual property can affect local artists as well, 
not just major artists and world-wide popular artists. 
I learned from this article that AI can create new problems for intellectual property because creators may not always know when their work is being used to train AI models. 
It also made me realize that governments and technology companies will need to find a way to allow AI to develop while still protecting the rights of creators.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this news article because it connects directly to our workshop discussion about intellectual property, copyright and AI. 
During the workshop, we discussed how creators have rights over their work, including how and where it can be used. 
we also discussed the recent news about Sony and Warner Music suing Anthropic over the alleged use of copyrighted music to train Claude, 
which showed me how these intellectual property issues are already affecting the music industry.

## Artefact 3: Copyright and AI training around the world

<img width="1900" height="1017" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f73b314f-3c8f-4e42-843c-bf03d8a52d2a" />
https://cqu.primo.exlibrisgroup.com/permalink/61CQU_INST/1eoclbh/cdi_springer_journals_10_1007_s40319_025_01569_6

### Summary of the artefact
This journal article by de la Durantaye (2025) examines how different countries are dealing with the use of copyrighted content to train generative AI. 
The research looks at the US, Canada, UK, EU, Israel, China, Singapore and Japan, focusing on issues such as control, compensation, transparency and legal certainty. 
The article explains that countries are trying to find a balance between supporting AI companies and protecting copyright holders.
I was surprised to learn that the copyright laws of different countries can affect the legal environment for AI companies and may be considered when deciding where to carry out AI training. 
However, the article explains that copyright law is not the decisive factor when AI companies choose where to locate their training facilities.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this artefact because after writing Artefact 2, I became curious about how different countries handle copyright when AI companies use copyrighted content to train their models.
While reading this article, I did not realise that countries could have such different approaches to the same intellectual property issue. 
I was especially surprised to learn that copyright laws can affect the legal environment for AI companies and may be considered when deciding where to conduct AI training. 
However, I also found it interesting that copyright law is not the main factor when companies choose their training locations.
This made me realize that intellectual property can have a much bigger impact on AI development than I originally thought it was. 
It also made me wonder how these laws will continue to change as AI becomes more advanced and how countries will balance the rights of creators with the development of AI.
