## Artefact 1: What is Censorship? 

<img width="1611" height="891" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8f263daf-b8b8-4ca7-afbd-52831a91391e" />

### Summary of the artefact
This article from Encyclopaedia Britannica, written by Anastaplo (2026), explains what censorship is, its history, and how it relates to freedom of speech and government authority. 
It explains censorship as changing, suppressing or prohibiting speech or writing that is considered harmful or against the common good (Anastaplo 2026). 
I was surprised to learn how far back censorship goes, as the article discusses examples from ancient Greece and Rome, ancient China and medieval societies.
Additionally, living in a world where we have a great deal of freedom, I found it interesting that the idea of freedom of speech developed in response to censorship. 
Anastaplo (2026) explains that the US Constitution drafted in 1787 anticipated protections for 'speech’ and ‘the press’ that was later included in the First Amendment. 
Overall, I learned that censorship is not only a modern issue involving the Internet or social media. It has existed for centuries 
and has always created tensions between authorities and people's freedom to express their ideas.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose this artefact because Encyclopaedia Britannica does not only explain what censorship is, but also provides a broad and generally neutral overview of its history and development.
During the workshop, I learnt that censorship existed even before the Internet was invented, which made me curious about how censorship worked in the past. 
Before this, the main form of censorship I knew was something more visible, such as television and film classifications. 
However, after the workshop and reading this article, I learnt that censorship can come in many different forms and can involve controlling or restricting people’s access to information, speech and ideas. Overall, this artefact helped me understand that censorship is much broader than simply blocking explicit content. 
It made me more aware that censorship can affect what people are allowed to see, hear or express, which also made me think more carefully about who should have the responsibility to decide what content should be restricted and who should take responsibility for censorship.

### Summary of the artefact
This YouTube video by TEDx Talks features David Shanks, who was New Zealand's Chief Censor, discussing when censorship may become necessary. 
He explained the difficulty of balancing freedom of expression with the responsibility to protect society from harmful content (TEDx Talks 2019). 
One example discussed in the video is the livestream of the Christchurch Mosque attack in 2019, which showed me that this kind of content can cause a huge amount of chaos when it is allowed to spread freely. 
He also stated that censorship should not simply be used because authorities disagree with something, and that freedom of expression should be protected whenever possible. Overall, he argues that censorship may become necessary when content is allowed to remain unrestricted and may create a serious risk of harm.

In contrast, another video by TEDx Talks features Olivia Orlewicz discussing her own experience with youth censorship.
When she was in 6th grade, she prepared a ted talk about veganism but was not allowed to present it to her classmates because the topic was considered as too mature (TEDx Talks 2020). 
What I found interesting was that the censorship she experienced was not about protecting young people from obviously harmful or explicit material. 
Instead, as a view, fact or idea that was withheld from students because adults decided that it was unsuitable for them. 
Orlewicz argues that allowing young people to discuss difficult subjects can create opportunities for educational discussion rather than simply preventing them from hearing different perspectives.

### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose these videos because I found both of them fascinating, and they further developed my curiosity after learning what censorship is and from the many things I learned during the workshop. 
I became more curious about who should actually have the responsibility to decide when something should be censored. <!-- Overview --> 
During the workshop, we went through multiple scenarios about who should be responsible for censorship. 
One of them involved roleplaying how censorship can be implemented as a government employee, as a CEO of a private company, and even as a member of society such as ourselves. 
One of the scenarios was, “What could be done to prevent the Mosque attack livestream from spreading as the government or social platform?” After researching about this, 
I found out that no users reported the video during the live broadcast (Rosen 2019). 
Facebook also explained that its AI was unable to automatically detect the livestream because AI systems need large amounts of similar training data to recognise certain types of content, 
and first-person footage of real violent events is fortunately very rare (Rosen 2019). It was also difficult for the system to distinguish the attack from visually similar but harmless content, 
such as livestreamed video games.

After the video started spreading, another problem was that people created edited and modified versions of it, which made automated detection even more difficult. 
This made me realise that deciding what should be restricted is not always very simple, and that technology cannot always handle censorship by itself. 
Without reports from users and human reviewers, AI may not recognise harmful content quickly enough. 
This also made me think that ordinary users have some responsibility to report harmful content rather than relying completely on governments or social media companies to find everything themselves. <!-- Workshop -->Overall, David Shanks' video helped me understand not only when censorship can sometimes be necessary, 
especially when content creates a serious risk of harm, but also why it should not simply be used because someone in authority disagrees with an idea. 
This made me think more carefully about how much responsibility governments and other authorities should have when deciding what people can see or share. 
It also made me realise that this responsibility should not fall on one group alone, 
because governments, social media companies and even ordinary users can all play a role in preventing harmful content from spreading. <!-- main video -->

On the other hand, Olivia Orlewicz's video gave me another perspective because the censorship she experienced happened in a school rather than through a government or online platform. 
I found it interesting that preventing her presentation may have stopped students from having an educational discussion about a difficult topic. <!-- olivia's -->
Comparing the two videos made me realise that the reason behind censorship is important. 
Protecting people from serious harm is different from preventing someone from sharing topics for educational purposes or expressing opinions simply because the topic may be uncomfortable for the audience.
Overall, these artefacts made me think that responsibility for censorship should not only be about who has the power to restrict content, but also about whether there is a reasonable reason for restricting it in the first place. Additionally, I believe that we also have our own shared responsibility when it comes to freedom of speech and censorship. <!-- conclusion / self reflect -->

### Summary of the artefact
This article by the Australian Human Rights Commission (2023) discusses the difficulty of controlling misinformation and disinformation online while still protecting people's freedom of expression. 
The article explains that social media has become an important place for people to share information, opinions and ideas, but it can also allow harmful or false information to spread. 
The Commission states that some level of restriction may be necessary when dealing with harmful misinformation and disinformation, but there should also be transparency and accountability when deciding what content should be restricted (Australian Human Rights Commission 2023).

On the other hand, eSafety Commissioner (2024) discusses the opposite problem, where there may not be enough moderation of harmful content online. 
The report found that X had significantly reduced its trust and safety staff, including safety engineers and content moderators. 
It also found that response times to reports of hateful content became slower after these reductions (eSafety Commissioner 2024). 
What I found interesting was that censorship and content moderation are not only about the risk of removing too much information, 
but also about what can happen when harmful content is not dealt with quickly enough. 
Overall, these two Australian sources showed me that online censorship and moderation require a balance between keeping users safe and protecting people's ability to express themselves.


### Justification on why I chose this artefact
I chose these two sources for this artefact because I think it is better to understand both sides of the issue rather than only focusing on one perspective. 
After researching the Christchurch attack and responsibility for censorship in Artefact 2, I became more curious about how Australia deals with similar problems involving harmful online content. 
During the workshop, we discussed how both governments and private companies can influence what information remains available online, which made me want to understand how this issue is approached in Australia.
The Australian Human Rights Commission article helped me understand one side of the issue, where restricting too much content can affect freedom of expression. 
I found it interesting that even when the purpose of censorship is to stop misinformation or harmful content, there still needs to be transparency and accountability about why something is being restricted. 
The Commission argues that harmful misinformation should be addressed without unnecessarily silencing reasonable disagreement, which reminded me of the youth censorship example in Artefact 2.
In Olivia Orlewicz’s case, preventing her presentation may have stopped students from hearing and discussing a different perspective simply because the topic was considered uncomfortable or too mature.	

On the other hand, the eSafety report showed me what could happen when a social media company may not have enough resources to moderate harmful content effectively. X reported major reductions in its trust and safety workforce, while eSafety also reported slower responses to users reporting hateful content. Similar to what I learnt from the Christchurch example in Artefact 2, harmful information can spread very quickly online, which means waiting too long to respond can also create risks for users.
Comparing these two sources made me realize that the ethical problem with censorship is not simply whether content should or should not be removed. If governments or companies restrict too much, they may interfere with freedom of expression. However, if they restrict too little or respond too slowly, harmful content may continue spreading and affect other people. Overall, these artefacts made me understand that governments and private companies both have responsibilities when dealing with online content, but their decisions should also have reasonable justification, transparency and accountability.
